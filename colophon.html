Materials & decisions / a working colophon

How this site is built

I wanted a place that could hold my artwork, education work, technical curiosity, and everyday interests together. A café gallery felt closer to that than a job title and a polished portrait.

A scene with room to read

The Leaf Café in Đà Nẵng is a reference for the warm plaster, dark wood, greenery, and small tile details. The page borrows that atmosphere without using the café’s photographs as decoration. My selected artwork stays in its original colors.

The scene suggests sitting inside a café and looking through frosted glass. The homepage uses a walnut-brown surround with warm paper and muted green reading surfaces, so the café interior sets the mood. Sparse jacaranda and bougainvillea branches belong behind the cards, with pale daylight visible between the leaves. I chose these plant references for their connection to Đà Nẵng and my Vietnamese heritage. They are stylized interpretations: fine divided jacaranda leaves and lilac blossoms, and pointed bougainvillea leaves with papery pink bracts. A blurred jacaranda shape suggests distance. The background leaves ample open space; the cards, captions, and text remain opaque. One separate bougainvillea branch enters from the right at the transition into animation and illustration. It stays still and is omitted on smaller screens so the work has room to breathe.

A flat wooden strip suggests the counter without a three-dimensional bevel or visible top plane. Each drink shares a center with its saucer. Matcha and Vietnamese egg coffee make the café reference more personal. A small olive-shaded lamp offers a visible source for the warmer evening light.

Useful before the first click

The introduction describes my practice as an artist, engineer, and educator, and links directly to contact information. It emphasizes the connections between disciplines rather than a snapshot of my employment. Cards include concrete examples of what I have worked on and explicit links to read further. The essential information should not depend on opening every story. Longer reflections, implementation details, and personal interests sit a level deeper for people who choose to explore.

The collection moves from a short AI perspective into education and professional accessibility work, then the combat prototype. Applied graphics follow: historical seal restoration, printed apparel, and a class jacket. Animation and illustration appear further down. This order makes relevant experience easy to find while leaving room for a wider artistic practice.

Typography as a practical choice

My typography class made me pay closer attention to how type shapes reading. Here, a Helvetica-based system stack gives the main text a quiet, familiar voice; Arial and the device’s sans-serif font are fallbacks. Small serif accents offer a different tone. This is a choice for this page, not a claim that serif type is inherently harder to read.

System fonts avoid a font download, but the exact appearance varies across devices. Texture sits behind the content rather than filtering letters or artwork.

A small static site

The implementation uses HTML, CSS, and two small JavaScript files for the story dialogs and café lighting. This collection does not currently need a framework or a build step. That keeps the source easy to inspect and makes static hosting on GitHub Pages a natural fit.

The tradeoff is manual maintenance: project stories live in the HTML, and the base stylesheet and gallery overrides are separate. If the collection grows, shared templates or a small static generator may become worthwhile.

Content first, dialogs second

Project cards are ordinary links to stories in the same document. JavaScript enhances them into native dialogs, gives the dialog its story heading, and returns focus to the opener when it closes. Without JavaScript or dialog support, the stories remain in the page. This colophon needs no JavaScript.

Skip links and visible focus styles are included. Reduced-motion preferences remove smooth scrolling and transitions, including changes in lighting; high-contrast modes remove decorative imagery. Decorative images have empty alternative text and are hidden from assistive technology. These are implemented features, not a completed accessibility certification.

What stays small

There are no third-party scripts, analytics, remote fonts, or autoplay media in the page. The site code does not set cookies. Artwork uses WebP; simple scene details use SVG. The frosted-window foliage is painted into one optimized WebP image. Its lower edge dissolves into CSS light and shade that extend through the collection. Irregular pools of warm daylight suggest openings between leaves. Their intensity and the visibility of the distant painting change with the time of day; the wood and paper keep the scene grounded. These layers stay behind opaque reading surfaces, and their edges fade into the surrounding texture. The painting appears once, without a live blur filter.

For now, there is no command palette, service worker, or live metrics widget. The navigation and content are small enough that those features would need a specific purpose before earning their place.

A café that follows the day

The first version uses a simple local-clock schedule: daylight from 7 a.m. to 5 p.m., twilight from 5 to 7 a.m. and 5 to 8 p.m., and lamplight overnight. This is an artistic rhythm, not a calculation of sunrise or weather. It needs no location access, network request, or saved preference. The native Café light selector offers a manual preview, with Auto restoring the schedule.

A small script checks the device clock at each minute boundary, pauses its timer while the page is hidden, and checks again when you return. Color and light opacity transition over 1.6 seconds when the mode changes; reduced-motion preferences make the change immediate. The branches, cards, and text do not move. Without JavaScript, the page stays in daylight and the unused control stays hidden.

The gallery uses still previews. Animation, drawing-process, and prototype clips play only when requested, with native video controls and no looping or autoplay. Each video uses preload="none"; closing a story stops its clip, and starting another pauses the previous one. Short excerpts keep attention on a specific scene or decision rather than making the whole page move.

The illustration’s process frames come from my timelapse. The game footage comes from the running prototype’s own viewport. Captions explain what the recordings show and distinguish navigation fields from combat positioning. These are examples to inspect, not performance or correctness scores.

Evidence, with its limits attached

The source check verifies local HTML and stylesheet references, fragment targets, duplicate IDs, and these uncompressed file-size budgets: 10 KiB for homepage JavaScript, 40 KiB for the two stylesheets together, and 100 KiB for the foliage background. These limits are project choices, not industry standards or loading-time scores.

The selected illustration has a 150 KiB budget. The four video excerpts have a combined 5 MiB budget and a 2 MiB limit per clip; they are optional media, not an initial page-download target. The source check also verifies posters, story destinations, and play-on-request video attributes.

Read the latest saved check report. This is a manually generated snapshot, not live deployment telemetry. It can be reproduced from the source with python scripts/check-site.py. Image sizes are file bytes; browser transfer sizes and caching may differ.

Full browser layout review, keyboard and screen-reader testing, and Lighthouse and automated accessibility audits are still outstanding for this revision. No scores or coverage percentages are claimed here. The next useful checks are small-screen readability, dialog focus behavior, and loading performance with the painted foliage background.

The draft changes and their history are available in the site’s pull request. A custom CI reporting pipeline has not been added.

Authorship and assistance

The Whig–Clio project restores existing identity artwork through historical research and vector reconstruction; it does not claim the original seals as my designs. The apparel graphics and Class of 2023 jacket design are mine. The jacket photograph is by Denise Applewhite, Princeton University Office of Communications, with a source link in its story. The Scully photograph documents the finished print.

The hotpot illustration and the visual artwork, animation, and compositing in Bloom are mine. The hotpot scene depicts characters from Zenless Zone Zero. The film excerpt is silent and makes no claim of soundtrack authorship. The wave-board study includes an identified reference to Hokusai’s The Great Wave off Kanagawa.

The game prototype uses third-party sprites and environment assets. That case study presents my system design, direction of AI-assisted development, and testing process, rather than claiming the game’s visual assets as my artwork.

I set the personal direction, choose references, and review and refine the site with AI assistance for code and writing. The foliage background and separate bougainvillea branch are AI-generated gouache-style decorations informed by my café photograph; the remaining scene details are code-based SVGs. It is not presented as my hand-painted artwork.

Part of the point of this page is to keep those distinctions visible. A finished-looking result is only one part of the work; the decisions, limitations, and revisions matter too.
