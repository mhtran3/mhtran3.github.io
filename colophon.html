Materials & decisions / a working colophon

How this site is built

I wanted a place that could hold my artwork, education work, technical curiosity, and everyday interests together. A café gallery felt closer to that than a job title and a polished portrait.

A scene with room to read

The Leaf Café in Đà Nẵng is a reference for the warm plaster, dark wood, greenery, and small tile details. The page borrows that atmosphere without using the café’s photographs as decoration. My selected artwork stays in its original colors.

The homepage is arranged like a corner of the café: a dark ceiling with small downlights over the header, an ochre plaster wall behind my name, a dark wooden table under the trees, and a slatted wooden counter for contact details at the bottom. The arrangement follows my photographs of The Leaf. The selected work lies on the table as one long folded sheet, like a zine: writing and pictures read together in one column, labels and roles sit in a narrow margin, pictures are printed on the page, and creases separate the parts. Sun falls through the leaves onto the table and the sheet in a few strong, warm patches, breaking up cool shade. Jacaranda and bougainvillea branches hang over the edges of the table. I chose these plant references for their connection to Đà Nẵng and my Vietnamese heritage. They are stylized interpretations: fine divided jacaranda leaves and lilac blossoms, and pointed bougainvillea leaves with papery pink bracts. The branches stay in the margins, pass behind the wall and the counter, and are omitted on smaller screens so the work has room to breathe.

The wall lamp is a trombone slide reused as a fixture, drawn in SVG after one at the café: two parallel tubes and a U-bend, a gooseneck, and the bell as its shade. Its cord is tied off at the bracket and runs over the pulley drawn on the lamp, then hangs free; the cord is a real button, so drag the weight down or press it to switch the lamp. It swings briefly when released, and brushing past it only stirs it slightly, at most every few seconds. The fixture sits fully below the ceiling in a reserved margin beside the introduction, with its wall bracket visible. A tight contact shadow and a shallow daylight shadow keep it connected to the plaster; the offset settles after dark, when the lamp casts its warm pool of light down the wall. The counter holds a single-group espresso machine in steel, walnut and brass, with a small “mt” nameplate as if it were the café’s own; press it to pull a shot. Beside it are iced matcha latte drawn from the one I ordered there, with cold foam, a matcha layer and a kraft straw on a dark coaster; egg coffee resting in a blue-and-white bowl of hot water, the way it is traditionally kept warm; and chè sương sa hạt lựu, pandan jelly and red water-chestnut pieces in coconut milk over crushed ice. All three share one eye level and one light direction, and each can be sipped and refilled. The counter ends where a real one would: the last stretch of countertop is a lift-up flap with hinges and a brass ring, and below it a short gate hangs from the counter’s end post, raised off the floor, opening onto the darker back of the café. Press it and it swings through and settles. It is left out on narrow screens, where the stacked contact details would make it implausibly tall. Some details only appear if you stay a while: after a few minutes the coffee stops steaming and the matcha glass gathers condensation.

Useful before the first click

The introduction describes my practice as an artist, engineer, and educator, and links directly to contact information. It emphasizes the connections between disciplines rather than a snapshot of my employment. Each entry on the sheet includes concrete examples of what I have worked on and a link to read further. The essential information should not depend on opening every story. Longer reflections, implementation details, and personal interests sit a level deeper for people who choose to explore.

The collection moves from a short AI perspective into education and professional accessibility work, then the combat prototype. Applied graphics follow: historical seal restoration, printed apparel, and a class jacket. Animation and illustration appear further down. This order makes relevant experience easy to find while leaving room for a wider artistic practice.

Typography with a story

My typography class made me pay closer attention to how type shapes reading. I chose Bricolage Grotesque for the headings partly because of its story. Designer Mathieu Triay connects its French and British influences to migration and belonging. That story resonated with me; its expressive shapes also bring character to the page.

Bricolage gives the name and the headings on the sheet a shared voice across disciplines. Fraunces, by Phaedra Charles and Flavia Zimbardi, appears at selected moments: on the sheet, the opening statement and the quote from my education work; inside the stories, the AI reflection’s title and the education story’s opening statement. Its softer serif forms mark a shift toward reflection. The distinction follows the role of a passage rather than dividing art from engineering.

Both use medium weight and automatic optical sizing. Fraunces uses moderate softness and its less irregular forms, with more open spacing than the Bricolage headings. Paragraphs and navigation keep a Helvetica-based system stack, with Arial and the device’s sans-serif font as fallbacks. This is a choice for this page, not a claim that serif type is inherently harder to read. Texture sits behind the content rather than filtering letters or artwork.

Both fonts are served from this site under the SIL Open Font License (Bricolage license; Fraunces license), with Latin, extended Latin, and Vietnamese character subsets. The browser downloads only the subsets needed for visible text and shows a system fallback while a font loads. Fraunces is not preloaded: on the enhanced homepage it is requested when one of its stories opens. Body text needs no font download; its exact appearance varies across devices.

A small static site

The implementation uses HTML, CSS, and three small JavaScript files: the story dialogs and the seal comparison, the café lighting, and the things you can touch. This collection does not currently need a framework or a build step. That keeps the source easy to inspect and makes static hosting on GitHub Pages a natural fit.

The tradeoff is manual maintenance: project stories live in the HTML, and the base stylesheet and gallery overrides are separate. If the collection grows, shared templates or a small static generator may become worthwhile.

Content first, dialogs second

Each entry’s title is an ordinary link to a story in the same document; the entry’s picture opens it too, without adding another keyboard stop. The seals are the exception: they move the comparison instead, and the comparison has its own keyboard stop, a range control. JavaScript enhances these links into native dialogs, gives the dialog its story heading, and returns focus to the opener when it closes. Without JavaScript or dialog support, the stories remain in the page. This colophon needs no JavaScript.

Skip links and visible focus styles are included. Reduced-motion preferences remove smooth scrolling, transitions, and the moving branches and light; an Eye candy switch stills every ambient movement for anyone else, and that one choice is remembered in the browser’s local storage; high-contrast modes remove decorative imagery. Decorative images have empty alternative text and are hidden from assistive technology. These are implemented features, not a completed accessibility certification.

What stays small

There are no third-party scripts, analytics, or remote fonts in the page. The site code does not set cookies. Artwork uses WebP; simple scene details use SVG. The table is a fixed layer behind the page: a colour for the wood in shade, four small images of sunlight, and a wood-grain texture generated in code. The sunlight images are alpha masks, so the stylesheet can paint them in whatever colour the sun has at that minute, and the same images serve the wood and the paper. Only position and opacity animate: each patch of sun rotates a degree or two around the point where its branch would meet the tree, on cycles of different lengths so they never fall into step.

For now, there is no command palette, service worker, or live metrics widget. The navigation and content are small enough that those features would need a specific purpose before earning their place.

A café that follows the day

The light follows the device clock continuously. A day is a set of hand-picked moments, including night, blue hour, sunrise, morning, noon, afternoon, golden hour and sunset. Between two moments, every colour is blended in the OKLab colour space so the transition looks even. The sun rises on the left at 6:00 and sets on the right at 18:45; its position shifts the patches of sun and sets the direction and length of the paper’s shadow. The lamps come on by themselves as it gets dark unless you have switched them. By day the table and the paper sit in warm brown shade, and sun comes through gaps in the leaves as bright patches: pale gold at noon, orange at golden hour. Each gap between leaves throws a small oval image of the sun’s disc, so a lone gap prints a dim oval and a crowd of gaps prints overlapping ovals that merge into a bright patch with a scalloped edge. The light images are built that way, from thousands of gaps convolved with the sun’s disc in a short script: a few stray ovals, and three openings in the canopy, each crossed by the sharp shapes of leaves close to the table. The pattern is fixed to the screen, so as the page scrolls, the sheet slides along the table while the light stays where it falls. On the sheet it sits a few pixels toward the light, because the paper is nearer the leaves than the wood, and the sheet’s shadow falls the other way. Each patch swings on its own uneven rhythm (16, 20 and 24 seconds), in time with one of the branches over the table, and moves identically on the wood and on the paper. After dark, lamplight pools in the middle of the table, a little from the downlights and more with each lamp switched on. The light sits beneath the sheet’s text and pictures, so it changes the paper and wood, never the ink, the photographs, or the illustrations. The two seals are a comparison: dragging across either one (or using the arrow keys) moves a line through both, with the image I was given on one side and my restoration on the other. The supplied image is cut, scaled and centred so each seal’s outer ring sits on the ring of its restoration. Both versions are ink on a transparent background, so the paper shows through them as if they were printed on it. Measured in Chromium against the darkest pixels behind every line of text on the sheet, at desktop and phone widths and at eleven times from before dawn through night (with the lamps on and off), every line stays at or above 4.8:1, above the 4.5:1 that small text needs. This is an artistic rhythm, not a calculation of sunrise or weather, and it needs no location access or network request. The only thing the page remembers is the Eye candy choice, kept in the browser’s local storage. A native range slider lets you scrub through the day, and “Back to now” returns to your clock.

The script updates every 30 seconds, stops while the page is hidden, and catches up when you return. It writes CSS custom properties; the stylesheet decides what each one touches. Text on the plaster switches between dark and light ink by measuring contrast, and around sunrise and sunset, when the wall passes through mid-tones, the plaster is nudged lighter or darker so small text keeps at least 6.2:1. Text never moves; only the light and the branches do. Without JavaScript, the page stays at midday, the lamps stay off, and the slider, cord and drinks are hidden rather than left unresponsive.

The heatmap preview and its copy inside the case study play silently and loop when at least 35% visible. Native controls let readers pause; a viewer pause is respected when scrolling away and back. Leaving view, hiding the tab, or opening another story pauses the clip. Reduced-motion preferences keep playback manual. Animation, drawing-process, and exploration-flow clips still play on request, and an automatic preview never interrupts a manually started clip. Every video uses preload="none"; closing a story stops and removes its video. If automatic playback is unavailable, the poster and Play control remain usable. Short excerpts keep attention on a specific scene or decision.

The illustration’s process frames come from my timelapse. The game footage comes from the running prototype’s own viewport. Captions explain what the recordings show and distinguish navigation fields from combat positioning. These are examples to inspect, not performance or correctness scores.

Evidence, with its limits attached

The source check verifies local HTML and stylesheet references, fragment targets, duplicate IDs, and uncompressed file-size budgets, including 32 KiB for homepage JavaScript, 69 KiB for the three stylesheets together, 224 KiB for the table and its light, 200 KiB for the two branches, 80 KiB for the seal comparison, 72 KiB for Bricolage, and 80 KiB for Fraunces. Bricolage totals about 65 KiB and Fraunces about 75 KiB across all subsets. Their Latin subsets are about 39 and 33 KiB respectively; other character subsets load only when needed. These limits are project choices, not industry standards or loading-time scores.

The selected illustration has a 150 KiB budget. The four unique video excerpts have a combined 5 MiB budget and a 2 MiB limit per clip. The heatmap loads when it comes into view; the other clips load on request. The source check also verifies posters, story destinations, native controls, and the muted, deferred-loading attributes for automatic previews.

Read the latest saved check report. This is a manually generated snapshot, not live deployment telemetry. It can be reproduced from the source with python scripts/check-site.py. Image sizes are file bytes; browser transfer sizes and caching may differ.

A headless Chromium review at desktop, tablet, and phone widths, at several times of day, found no script errors or sideways scrolling; each entry is one keyboard stop, and story dialogs return focus to the title that opened them. Screen-reader testing on real devices, and Lighthouse and automated accessibility audits, are still outstanding. No scores or coverage percentages are claimed here. The next useful checks are small-screen readability and loading and scrolling performance on real devices.

The draft changes and their history are available in the site’s pull request. A custom CI reporting pipeline has not been added.

Authorship and assistance

The Whig–Clio project restores existing identity artwork through historical research and vector reconstruction; it does not claim the original seals as my designs. The apparel graphics and Class of 2023 jacket design are mine. The jacket photograph is by Denise Applewhite, Princeton University Office of Communications, with a source link in its story. The Scully photograph documents the finished print.

The hotpot illustration and the visual artwork, animation, and compositing in Bloom are mine. The hotpot scene depicts characters from Zenless Zone Zero. The film excerpt is silent and makes no claim of soundtrack authorship. The wave-board study includes an identified reference to Hokusai’s The Great Wave off Kanagawa.

The game prototype uses third-party sprites and environment assets. That case study presents my system design, direction of AI-assisted development, and testing process, rather than claiming the game’s visual assets as my artwork.

I set the personal direction, choose references, and review and refine the site with AI assistance for code and writing. The foliage background and separate bougainvillea branch are AI-generated gouache-style decorations informed by my café photograph; the remaining scene details are code-based SVGs. It is not presented as my hand-painted artwork.

Part of the point of this page is to keep those distinctions visible. A finished-looking result is only one part of the work; the decisions, limitations, and revisions matter too.
