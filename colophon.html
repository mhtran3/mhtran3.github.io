Materials & decisions / a working colophon

How this site is built

I wanted a place that could hold my artwork, education work, technical curiosity, and everyday interests together. A café gallery felt closer to that than a job title and a polished portrait.

A scene with room to read

The Leaf Café in Đà Nẵng is a reference for the warm plaster, dark wood, greenery, and small tile details. The page borrows that atmosphere without using the café’s photographs as decoration. My selected artwork stays in its original colors.

The homepage is arranged like a corner of the café: a dark ceiling with small downlights over the header, an ochre plaster wall behind my name, a frosted window behind the collection, and a slatted wooden counter for contact details at the bottom. The arrangement follows my photographs of The Leaf, especially one looking up past a lit wall lamp toward leaves in a skylight. Sparse jacaranda and bougainvillea branches belong behind the cards, with pale daylight visible between the leaves. I chose these plant references for their connection to Đà Nẵng and my Vietnamese heritage. They are stylized interpretations: fine divided jacaranda leaves and lilac blossoms, and pointed bougainvillea leaves with papery pink bracts. A blurred jacaranda shape suggests distance. The background leaves ample open space; the cards, captions, and text remain opaque. One separate bougainvillea branch enters from the right at the transition into animation and illustration. It sways with the same breeze and is omitted on smaller screens so the work has room to breathe.

The wall lamp is a trombone slide reused as a fixture, drawn in SVG after one at the café: two parallel tubes and a U-bend, a gooseneck, and the bell as its shade. Its cord is tied off at the bracket and runs over the pulley drawn on the lamp, then hangs free; the cord is a real button, so drag the weight down or press it to switch the lamp. It swings briefly when released, and brushing past it only stirs it slightly, at most every few seconds. At night it throws a cone of light down the plaster, and its shadow falls on the wall away from whatever light is strongest. The counter holds a single-group espresso machine in steel, walnut and brass, with a small “mt” nameplate as if it were the café’s own; press it to pull a shot. Beside it are iced matcha latte drawn from the one I ordered there, with cold foam, a matcha layer and a kraft straw on a dark coaster; egg coffee resting in a blue-and-white bowl of hot water, the way it is traditionally kept warm; and chè sương sa hạt lựu, pandan jelly and red water-chestnut pieces in coconut milk over crushed ice. All three share one eye level and one light direction, and each can be sipped and refilled. The counter ends where a real one would: the last stretch of countertop is a lift-up flap with hinges and a brass ring, and below it a short gate hangs from the counter’s end post, raised off the floor, opening onto the darker back of the café. Press it and it swings through and settles. It is left out on narrow screens, where the stacked contact details would make it implausibly tall. Some details only appear if you stay a while: after a few minutes the coffee stops steaming and the matcha glass gathers condensation.

Useful before the first click

The introduction describes my practice as an artist, engineer, and educator, and links directly to contact information. It emphasizes the connections between disciplines rather than a snapshot of my employment. Cards include concrete examples of what I have worked on and explicit links to read further. The essential information should not depend on opening every story. Longer reflections, implementation details, and personal interests sit a level deeper for people who choose to explore.

The collection moves from a short AI perspective into education and professional accessibility work, then the combat prototype. Applied graphics follow: historical seal restoration, printed apparel, and a class jacket. Animation and illustration appear further down. This order makes relevant experience easy to find while leaving room for a wider artistic practice.

Typography with a story

My typography class made me pay closer attention to how type shapes reading. I chose Bricolage Grotesque for the headings partly because of its story. Designer Mathieu Triay connects its French and British influences to migration and belonging. That story resonated with me; its expressive shapes also bring character to the page.

Bricolage gives the name, section headings, and project cards a shared voice across disciplines. Fraunces, by Phaedra Charles and Flavia Zimbardi, appears at selected moments inside the stories: the AI reflection’s title and the education story’s opening statement. Its softer serif forms mark a shift toward reflection. The distinction follows the role of a passage rather than dividing art from engineering.

Both use medium weight and automatic optical sizing. Fraunces uses moderate softness and its less irregular forms, with more open spacing than the Bricolage headings. Paragraphs and navigation keep a Helvetica-based system stack, with Arial and the device’s sans-serif font as fallbacks. This is a choice for this page, not a claim that serif type is inherently harder to read. Texture sits behind the content rather than filtering letters or artwork.

Both fonts are served from this site under the SIL Open Font License (Bricolage license; Fraunces license), with Latin, extended Latin, and Vietnamese character subsets. The browser downloads only the subsets needed for visible text and shows a system fallback while a font loads. Fraunces is not preloaded: on the enhanced homepage it is requested when one of its stories opens. Body text needs no font download; its exact appearance varies across devices.

A small static site

The implementation uses HTML, CSS, and two small JavaScript files for the story dialogs and café lighting. This collection does not currently need a framework or a build step. That keeps the source easy to inspect and makes static hosting on GitHub Pages a natural fit.

The tradeoff is manual maintenance: project stories live in the HTML, and the base stylesheet and gallery overrides are separate. If the collection grows, shared templates or a small static generator may become worthwhile.

Content first, dialogs second

Project cards are ordinary links to stories in the same document. JavaScript enhances them into native dialogs, gives the dialog its story heading, and returns focus to the opener when it closes. Without JavaScript or dialog support, the stories remain in the page. This colophon needs no JavaScript.

Skip links and visible focus styles are included. Reduced-motion preferences remove smooth scrolling, transitions, and the moving branches and light; an Eye candy switch stills every ambient movement for anyone else, and that one choice is remembered in the browser’s local storage; high-contrast modes remove decorative imagery. Decorative images have empty alternative text and are hidden from assistive technology. These are implemented features, not a completed accessibility certification.

What stays small

There are no third-party scripts, analytics, remote fonts, or autoplay media in the page. The site code does not set cookies. Artwork uses WebP; simple scene details use SVG. The frosted window is a fixed layer behind the page, built from one painting split into a distant canopy and two nearer branches, plus two small textures of sunlight. Each layer is blurred ahead of time, so the browser never recomputes blur. Only position and opacity animate: branches rotate a degree or two around the point where they would meet the tree, on cycles of different lengths so they never fall into step, and the two light layers drift against each other so the overlapping patches shimmer. Everything stays behind opaque reading surfaces.

For now, there is no command palette, service worker, or live metrics widget. The navigation and content are small enough that those features would need a specific purpose before earning their place.

A café that follows the day

The light follows the device clock continuously. A day is a set of hand-picked moments, including night, blue hour, sunrise, morning, noon, afternoon, golden hour and sunset. Between two moments, every colour is blended in the OKLab colour space so the transition looks even. The sun rises on the left at 6:00 and sets on the right at 18:45; its position moves the bright patch on the glass, the dapples, and the direction and length of every card’s shadow. The lamps come on by themselves as it gets dark unless you have switched them. By day the light comes from a skylight overhead, through leaves, and lands on the cards and on the glass behind them. It is one pattern fixed to the room: soft shade from the distant canopy, a few leaves close enough to cast sharp shadows, and round patches of sun (each a small image of the sun through a gap). On the cards the pattern sits shifted toward the light, because they are nearer the skylight than the glass; with each card’s shadow falling the other way, that offset is what makes the cards seem to stand forward. As the page scrolls, the cards slide through the light while the pattern stays put. The distant canopy drifts slowly, and each of three near branches swings from its own stem on its own uneven rhythm (16, 20 and 24 seconds), so the light never moves as one sheet; two of them keep time with the jacaranda and bougainvillea in the window. A branch’s shadow moves identically on the glass and on every card. Each branch is a separate small image cropped to its leaves, so the browser only moves the pixels that hold a shadow. Behind each line of card text, a slight feathered blur softens whatever passes beneath, so the words never sit on a hard moving edge; away from the text the shadows stay sharp. The shade and the sun are separate layers: shade uses a soft-light blend and is kept modest; sun uses a screen blend that only brightens, so it can be strong without costing dark text any contrast. Both sit beneath each card’s text and images inside an isolated layer, so they change the paper and wood, never the ink or the artwork. Measured in Chromium behind every line of card text at 10:00, 12:40 and 17:30, text stays at or above 5:1. This is an artistic rhythm, not a calculation of sunrise or weather, and it needs no location access or network request. The only thing the page remembers is the Eye candy choice, kept in the browser’s local storage. A native range slider lets you scrub through the day, and “Back to now” returns to your clock.

The script updates every 30 seconds, stops while the page is hidden, and catches up when you return. It writes CSS custom properties; the stylesheet decides what each one touches. Text on the plaster switches between dark and light ink by measuring contrast, and around sunrise and sunset, when the wall passes through mid-tones, the plaster is nudged lighter or darker so small text keeps at least 4.6:1. Cards and text never move; only the scene behind them does. Without JavaScript, the page stays at midday, the lamps stay off, and the slider, cord and drinks are hidden rather than left unresponsive.

The gallery uses still previews. Animation, drawing-process, and prototype clips play only when requested, with native video controls and no looping or autoplay. Each video uses preload="none"; closing a story stops its clip, and starting another pauses the previous one. Short excerpts keep attention on a specific scene or decision rather than making the whole page move.

The illustration’s process frames come from my timelapse. The game footage comes from the running prototype’s own viewport. Captions explain what the recordings show and distinguish navigation fields from combat positioning. These are examples to inspect, not performance or correctness scores.

Evidence, with its limits attached

The source check verifies local HTML and stylesheet references, fragment targets, duplicate IDs, and uncompressed file-size budgets, including 28 KiB for homepage JavaScript, 69 KiB for the three stylesheets together, 300 KiB for the window layers, 72 KiB for Bricolage, and 80 KiB for Fraunces. Bricolage totals about 65 KiB and Fraunces about 75 KiB across all subsets. Their Latin subsets are about 39 and 33 KiB respectively; other character subsets load only when needed. These limits are project choices, not industry standards or loading-time scores.

The selected illustration has a 150 KiB budget. The four video excerpts have a combined 5 MiB budget and a 2 MiB limit per clip; they are optional media, not an initial page-download target. The source check also verifies posters, story destinations, and play-on-request video attributes.

Read the latest saved check report. This is a manually generated snapshot, not live deployment telemetry. It can be reproduced from the source with python scripts/check-site.py. Image sizes are file bytes; browser transfer sizes and caching may differ.

A headless Chromium review at desktop and phone widths, in all three lights, found no script errors or sideways scrolling, and story dialogs return focus to the card that opened them. Keyboard and screen-reader testing on real devices, and Lighthouse and automated accessibility audits, are still outstanding. No scores or coverage percentages are claimed here. The next useful checks are small-screen readability, dialog focus behavior, and loading performance with the painted foliage background.

The draft changes and their history are available in the site’s pull request. A custom CI reporting pipeline has not been added.

Authorship and assistance

The Whig–Clio project restores existing identity artwork through historical research and vector reconstruction; it does not claim the original seals as my designs. The apparel graphics and Class of 2023 jacket design are mine. The jacket photograph is by Denise Applewhite, Princeton University Office of Communications, with a source link in its story. The Scully photograph documents the finished print.

The hotpot illustration and the visual artwork, animation, and compositing in Bloom are mine. The hotpot scene depicts characters from Zenless Zone Zero. The film excerpt is silent and makes no claim of soundtrack authorship. The wave-board study includes an identified reference to Hokusai’s The Great Wave off Kanagawa.

The game prototype uses third-party sprites and environment assets. That case study presents my system design, direction of AI-assisted development, and testing process, rather than claiming the game’s visual assets as my artwork.

I set the personal direction, choose references, and review and refine the site with AI assistance for code and writing. The foliage background and separate bougainvillea branch are AI-generated gouache-style decorations informed by my café photograph; the remaining scene details are code-based SVGs. It is not presented as my hand-painted artwork.

Part of the point of this page is to keep those distinctions visible. A finished-looking result is only one part of the work; the decisions, limitations, and revisions matter too.
